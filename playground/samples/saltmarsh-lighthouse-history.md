# The Saltmarsh lighthouse: a short local history

The lighthouse at Saltmarsh Point stands on a spit of shingle at the mouth of the river Aldwen, about four kilometres east of the
village. It is 31 metres tall, painted in red and white bands, and visitors can climb its 142 steps on summer weekends.

## Why it was built

In the winter of 1851 three fishing boats and a coastal trading ship called the Margaret Ellis were wrecked on the sandbanks off
the point within a single month, and eleven people drowned. The village petitioned the trade authority for a light. Because the
coast was already well lit further north, the request was refused twice. In 1858 the village raised 900 pounds by subscription
and the authority finally agreed to build the lighthouse itself for a further 3,400 pounds.

## Building and first light

The tower was designed by the engineer Harold Thistlewood and built from local brick covered in cement render. The foundation
sits on 60 timber piles driven into the shingle, which the villagers called the forest underneath. Work began in March 1859 and
the lamp was first lit on 14 October 1860. The first keeper, Edmund Farrow, served for 22 years. His logbooks, which survive in the
village archive, record the weather, the passing ships and, each morning, how much paraffin was used.

The light was originally a fixed white lamp with a range of 12 nautical miles. In 1902 it was replaced with a rotating lens that
flashed every 10 seconds, so that sailors could tell Saltmarsh from the fixed light at Marrick Head.

## The keepers and their families

Three keepers shared the work in shifts, and their families lived in a row of cottages at the foot of the tower. The school
register of 1888 lists nine children from the lighthouse cottages, who walked four kilometres each way along the sea wall. In
the great storm of January 1894 the sea wall was breached, and for six days the families were cut off. The head keeper's wife,
Ruth Calloway, is remembered for baking bread each morning for all the cottages with flour that had been stored in the lamp room.

## Change and automation

Electricity arrived in 1936, and the paraffin lamp was replaced by an electric lamp of 1,000 watts. During the Second World War the
light was switched off, and the tower was used as a lookout post. The last resident keeper, Arthur Penhallow, left in 1974 when the
light was automated, and the cottages were sold. Today the light is controlled from a station 60 kilometres away and runs on a
modern LED lamp that uses less than 100 watts.

## The lighthouse today

A volunteer group, the Friends of Saltmarsh Light, took over the tower's upkeep in 1998. They raised 210,000 pounds, with help from
a heritage grant, to repair the cracked render and restore the lens room. The tower is open on Saturdays and Sundays from 1 May
to 30 September, between 11:00 and 16:00. Entry costs 4 pounds for adults and is free for children under 12. A small museum in
the old oil store shows the logbooks, a model of the Margaret Ellis and photographs of the families who lived there.

The annual lighthouse day is held on the second Saturday of August. There is a lifeboat demonstration at 14:00, a fish supper
on the green and, at dusk, the lamp is lit for a few minutes with the old paraffin burner, as it was in 1860.
